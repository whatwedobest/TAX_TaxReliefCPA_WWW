IRS Audit Representation

You have the legal right to have a CPA represent you before the IRS. You never have to speak to an IRS agent directly — and with Ronald A. Muscarella handling your audit, you won't have to.

Taxpayer Rights · IRC §7521

What Is IRS Audit Representation?

Under IRC §7521(b), you have the absolute right to suspend any IRS interview and consult with a CPA, attorney, or enrolled agent before responding to any question. When you are represented, the IRS must direct all communications to your representative — not to you.

An IRS audit is a formal examination of your tax return to verify that income, deductions, and credits are accurate and properly documented. The IRS audits returns through three methods:

  • Correspondence Audit — The most common type. The IRS sends a letter (typically a CP2000 or Letter 566) requesting documentation for specific items on your return. These must be responded to within the stated deadline or the IRS will assess additional tax automatically.
  • Office Audit — You are asked to bring records to an IRS Taxpayer Assistance Center. Scope is typically limited to the issues identified in the initial appointment letter.
  • Field Audit — An IRS Revenue Agent comes to your home or business. Field audits are the most comprehensive and are often conducted for self-employed individuals, small businesses, or returns with large Schedule C deductions.

Why You Should Never Respond to the IRS Alone

  • Any statement you make can be used to expand the scope of the audit to other years or issues
  • Providing more documentation than requested can open new audit issues
  • IRS agents are not required to inform you of deductions you may be entitled to claim
  • Deadline extensions must be formally requested — missing them can result in automatic assessments
  • Taxpayer confusion or nervousness can be misinterpreted as evasion
  • Agreeing to an assessment without understanding your appeal rights waives those rights permanently

A CP2000 is an automated IRS notice proposing additional tax because the IRS's records (from W-2s, 1099s, and other third-party information returns) do not match what you reported. It is technically a "correspondence examination" — not a formal audit — but it carries the same stakes. If you agree with the proposed changes, you respond with payment or payment arrangement. If you disagree, you must respond with documentation within the deadline or the IRS will automatically assess the proposed tax. Do not ignore a CP2000.
