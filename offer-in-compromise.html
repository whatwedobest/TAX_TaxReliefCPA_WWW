Offer in Compromise

Settle your IRS tax debt for less than the full amount owed — legally, permanently, and with a licensed CPA negotiating on your behalf.

IRS Program · IRC §7122

What Is an Offer in Compromise?

An Offer in Compromise (OIC) is an agreement between a taxpayer and the IRS that allows the taxpayer to settle their outstanding tax liability for less than the full amount owed. It is authorized under Internal Revenue Code §7122 and governed by IRS regulations at 26 C.F.R. §301.7122.

The IRS will accept an OIC when it determines that the offered amount represents the most the agency can reasonably expect to collect — based on the taxpayer's assets, income, and allowable expenses. When properly structured and submitted, an accepted OIC resolves the tax debt permanently: the IRS removes the federal tax lien once the settlement is paid, and you are not required to pay the balance that was forgiven.

This is not a loophole. It is an established IRS program — but acceptance rates are low for self-represented taxpayers because the IRS scrutinizes every financial detail. A CPA who understands IRS Collection Financial Standards, Reasonable Collection Potential (RCP) calculations, and dissipated asset rules is essential to a successful submission.

The Three OIC Grounds

  • Doubt as to Collectibility — the most common basis. You cannot pay the full debt within the remaining collection statute (generally 10 years from assessment under IRC §6502).
  • Doubt as to Liability — you dispute the legal validity of the assessed tax. No Form 656-L is required if the tax was established by a Tax Court decision.
  • Effective Tax Administration — you could theoretically pay, but collection would create economic hardship or be inequitable given exceptional circumstances.

Eligibility

Do You Qualify for an Offer in Compromise?

The IRS uses a specific formula to determine your Reasonable Collection Potential. Understanding that formula is the first step.

Basic Eligibility Requirements

  • All required federal tax returns have been filed (or will be filed before submission)
  • All required estimated tax payments for the current year are current
  • You are not in an open bankruptcy proceeding
  • You have received a bill (Notice of Deficiency or Notice and Demand)
  • Your $205 application fee is paid, or you qualify for the Low Income Certification waiver

The IRS pre-qualifier tool exists, but it uses simplified inputs. Ronald A. Muscarella performs a full manual RCP calculation — accounting for all IRS Collection Financial Standards, vehicle allowances, housing/utility limits by county, and legitimate dissipated asset exclusions — before determining whether an OIC is the right strategy for your situation.

Factors That Strengthen Your Offer

  • Low net equity in real estate and personal assets
  • Income at or near IRS allowable expense thresholds
  • Irregular or uncertain future income (self-employment, commission)
  • Advanced age or medical conditions reducing future earning capacity
  • Tax debt that is primarily from periods many years ago
  • Remaining collection statute period of fewer than five years

Clients who owe $50,000–$250,000 and earn moderate incomes are often strong OIC candidates — but each case is unique. The offer amount is the sum of your net realizable equity in assets plus your future income multiplied by 12 (lump-sum) or 24 (periodic payment).

Common Questions

Offer in Compromise FAQs

The IRS typically takes 6–12 months to process an Offer in Compromise. During the review period, the IRS collection statute of limitations is suspended. If your offer is rejected, you have 30 days to appeal to the IRS Office of Appeals, which can add several additional months. Ronald A. Muscarella manages every communication and deadline so nothing falls through the cracks.

Under IRS policy, most active collection activity — including levies — is suspended while a properly submitted OIC is under review and for 30 days after rejection (longer if you file an appeal). However, an existing Notice of Federal Tax Lien is not released during this period. We file Form 2848 (Power of Attorney) to intercept all IRS communications directly.

There is no statutory minimum. The offer amount must equal or exceed your Reasonable Collection Potential — the sum of net realizable equity in all assets plus future income. For a lump-sum cash offer (paid within 5 months), the future income multiplier is 12 months. For a periodic payment offer (6–24 months), the multiplier is 24 months. This formula can produce offers of a few thousand dollars on debts of six figures when the financial profile is right.

The IRS accepts roughly 30–40% of all OICs submitted each year, but acceptance rates for professionally prepared offers are significantly higher than for pro se submissions. The most common reasons for rejection are incomplete documentation, failure to account for all income sources, and offers below the IRS-calculated RCP. Our practice has a strong track record because we only submit offers we believe are supported by the numbers.

Home equity is included in the asset calculation, but the IRS uses quick-sale value (typically 80% of fair market value) minus any mortgage balance. For many Fort Lauderdale and Broward County homeowners whose properties are heavily mortgaged, net equity is low — which supports a lower offer. We conduct a full real property analysis as part of every case evaluation.

A rejection is not the end. You have 30 days to request a conference with the IRS Office of Appeals — an independent IRS function that reviews rejections fresh. If Appeals also sustains the rejection, other resolution options such as an installment agreement or Currently Not Collectible status may still be available. We evaluate every alternative from the start so you always have a contingency plan.
